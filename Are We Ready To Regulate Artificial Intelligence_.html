Are We Ready To Regulate Artificial Intelligence?

By Ayesha Haq, Ohio Lawyer, February 21, 2025
  • Share

For those who haven’t heard, artificial intelligence (AI) has taken the world by storm. Businesses and specialized professions have all been impacted in some way or another by AI. While AI has added great value to business functions and operations, it has also negatively impacted users who fall trap to circulation of misinformation and breaches of data privacy. Advancement in technology always comes with a set of pitfalls that need to be addressed early on. This is where lawmakers and governmental authorities come into play to effectively regulate the negative effects of technology.

Machine Learning vs. Generative AI

While there are different types of AI floating around, the basic model upon which the concept of AI rests is machine learning (ML), where a tool is taught to perform a specific action using the data or knowledge that is provided to it. ML enables organizations to use the technology to perform a specific task and use new data to improve its ability to perform that same task, for example, a software tool automatically generating reports once a set of data is provided to it.

Generative AI, on the other hand, is closer in nature to what we perceive as artificial intelligence. Generative AI uses a dataset to come up with new ideas and concepts, like new text, images and videos. For example, if you give an AI tool a prompt to create a commercial for a specific product, you’re using generative AI. This kind of AI, while useful, can prove to be the most detrimental in protecting our basic privacy rights.

Why Regulate?

Generative AI allows for malicious actors to feed a set of personal information to a tool and create misleading photos or videos which can cause severe harm to a person. With so much information on the internet, it is difficult to discern whether content has been created by AI or not. Many social media sites have encouraged users to label their content as AI generated as a way to put other users on notice. But is that enough? If generative AI has the ability to significantly harm an individual, it is safe to say that lawmakers need to actively think about regulating AI.

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act

Countries around the globe have started thinking about regulating AI through different active and pending legislation. The European Union is one of the first to come out with a more coherent framework. The EU AI Act, which was adopted on May 21, 2024, aims to standardize rules for AI usage and guidance on managing risks associated with AI. Article 2 of the EU AI Act defines the scope and lists out the types of persons or organizations this regulation applies to:1

1. Providers located within the EU: Defined as a natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body that develops an AI system or model and places it on the market for free or at a charge.

2. Deployers located within the EU: Defined as any natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body using an AI system under its authority (excludes AI systems used for a personal non-professional activity).

3. Providers and deployers outside the EU.

4. Importers: Defined as any natural or legal person located or established in the union that places on the market an AI system that bears the name or trademark of a natural or legal person established outside the EU.

5. Distributors: Defined as any natural or legal person in the supply chain, other than the provider or the importer, that makes an AI system available on the union market.

6. Product Manufacturers: Defined as any natural or legal person that places on the market or puts into service an AI system together with their product under their own name or trademark.

7. Authorized Representatives: Defined as any natural or legal person located or established in the EU who has received and accepted a written mandate from a Provider of an AI system or model to perform and carry out on its behalf the obligations and procedures mentioned in the act.

Classification System

Completely restricting the use of AI would be impractical for organizations trying to evolve themselves with new technology. Unlike the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) (which revolves around prohibiting the misuse of personal information) the EU AI Act lists scenarios and circumstances that would give rise to a prohibition of AI usage. The AI Act classifies these scenarios in a tiered system as follows:

1. Prohibited Systems.

a. “Subliminal, manipulative, or deceptive techniques” used to alter behavior and affect decision making, thereby causing significant harm.

b. “Exploiting vulnerabilities” such as that of race, age, disability or financial circumstances as a way to alter behavior which may cause significant harm.

c. “Biometric categorization systems” which may incorrectly infer sensitive information about an individual’s race, political and religious beliefs, sexual orientation except for where such information is required to be categorized by law enforcement.

d. “Social scoring” i.e., discrimination through classification of an individual’s personal traits or social behavior.

e. “Assessing the risk of an individual committing criminal offenses” which is solely based on profiling or personality traits.

f. “Compiling facial recognition databases.”

g. “Inferring emotions in workplaces or educational institutions.”

h. “Real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement” (exceptions apply).2

2. High-Risk AI Systems.

AI system is being used as a safety component of a product or the AI system is a product covered under legislation that falls under the Union Harmonisation Legislation.3

3. Limited Risk AI Systems.

These systems are subject to more lenient transparency requirements such that developers and deployers must ensure that the end-users are aware that they are interacting with AI.

4. Minimal Risk.

This is unregulated at the moment and includes AI enabled video games and spam filters. But the legal landscape is subject to change with the evolution of generative AI.

How Can We Comply?

As was the case with GDPR, organizations in the U.S. must be equipped to ensure compliance with the new AI regulation. Businesses these days are global and in order to succeed in the international market, they – and their lawyers – must be prepared to face the legal obligations that arise as a result of a commercial transaction. The easiest way to comply is to put your business clients on notice for prohibited and high-risk AI systems. Prohibited systems should not be used at any cost until and unless it is absolutely required. If that is the case, you may need to evaluate whether residents of the EU are being affected as a result and do an internal risk assessment. For high-risk systems, the EU has provided a framework for organizations to follow in order to ensure minimum impact. High-risk AI providers must:4 

1. Establish a risk management system. Whenever dealing with personal or sensitive business information, it is imperative that your organization has a proper risk management procedure in place, which is reviewed at least annually.

2. Draft technical documentation. This can be in the form of a publicly available privacy policy and terms of service or even contractual language that explains how your organization uses AI and what kind of data is processed.

3. Ensure that high-risk AI systems have a record-keeping or logging mechanism. This is to ensure transparency and accountability of AI systems and the output it generates.

4. Implement a way for the system to have human oversight. Having a human in the loop adds a second layer of validation to ensure that the output is accurate and does not create biased or discriminatory outcome.

5. Implement security measures to ensure that the system is accurate, robust and secure from outside threats. Security measures enable compliance with privacy and confidentiality requirements when handling sensitive information.

6. Establish a quality management system for compliance purposes. This can be any form of auditing procedure that ensures that your AI systems are in compliance with the requisite industry standard regulatory requirements.

It is my prediction that the EU AI Act will serve as the gold standard for future AI regulations, as the GDPR has been for privacy regulations across the globe. Lawyers and lawmakers in the U.S. should ensure that they fully understand how the EU AI Act interoperates with internal processes of U.S. businesses in order to provide effective counsel and legal advice. This article serves as a high-level summary of the regulation but there is still a great deal that needs to be parsed through. Chapters I and II of the regulation took full effect last year. Now is the time to understand the legal implications of using AI and the effect it has on the business of our clientele.

*Note: As of this writing, the United States has gotten up to speed with AI regulations by passing state legislation in Utah, Colorado, California, Illinois and New York, to name a few.

About the Author

Ayesha Haq is a senior associate in-house counsel for EZO, where she advises on matters pertaining to data privacy, particularly on regulations such as CCPA, HIPAA and GDPR. Her practice at EZO includes, but is not limited to, contract management, corporate law, employment law and trademark law. In addition to being a practicing attorney, she is a CQI IRCA certified Information Security Management System Lead Auditor which qualifies her to manage the information security side of the business at EZO. She earned her undergraduate degree from the University of Toronto and her law degree from the University of Cincinnati College of Law.
